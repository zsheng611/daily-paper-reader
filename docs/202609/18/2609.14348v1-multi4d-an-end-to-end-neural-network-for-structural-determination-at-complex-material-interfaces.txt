Abstract
Heterogeneous interfaces dictate the performance and degradation of functional materials, making it
essential to link local structural variations with macroscopic failure mechanisms to guide future mate-
rials design. Yet structural heterogeneity, phase overlap, and local disorder produce highly convoluted
diffraction signatures, making extended transition regions difficult to interpret at atomic resolution
across large fields of view. Here, we introduce Multi4D, a physics-informed neural network frame-
work for automated multi-component crystallographic identification using four-dimensional scanning
transmission electron microscopy (4D-STEM). By combining a latent-space Diffusion Transformer
for physics-constrained style translation with a rotation-invariant convolutional neural network for
orientation-agnostic classification, this approach translates multi-components diffraction datasets into
deterministic crystallographic maps with 98.82% accuracy. In addition, we introduce Diffraction-
Inferred Structural Complexity as an information-theoretic entropy metric derived from classifier
predictive uncertainty that quantifies local structural ambiguity. We apply Multi4D to generate high-
fidelity structural maps of complex superconducting heterostructures, corroded alloy surfaces, and
degraded solid-state battery interfaces down to single-nanometer spatial resolution. This framework
establishes a statistically robust analytical paradigm for automated microscopy, facilitating both
industrial quality control and the data-driven discovery of interfacial design principles.
Keywords: Four-dimensional scanning transmission electron microscopy, Simulation-to-experiment domain
adaptation, Rotation-invariant neural networks, Predictive uncertainty

Introduction
The
performance,
degradation
kinetics,
and
longevity of advanced functional materials are
fundamentally governed by atomic-scale hetero-
geneities at their complex interface[1, 2] For
instance,
in
advanced
semiconductor
device,
buried interfaces determine the electronic trans-
port, charge carrier density, and overall device
functionality or stability[3–5]. Similarly, in struc-
tural alloys, localized corrosion evolution that is
strongly shaped by nanoscale chemical and micro-
structural heterogeneity governs pit growth and
can promote crack initiation[6]. Because macro-
scopic performance and failure modes originate
at these internal boundaries, establishing predic-
tive structure–property relationships require the
precise identification of the underlying microstruc-
tural constituents at atomic level[1, 2]. However,
characterizing these regions is exceedingly chal-
lenging due to the highly heterogeneous nature
of complex interfaces, which often contain over-
lapping crystalline domains or chemically distinct
constituents[1–3]. Therefore, probing these buried
and heterogeneous interfaces with high spatial and
structural sensitivity remains a critical objective
and foster continuous advancements in character-
ization tools across both academia and industry.
An ideal characterization modality must simul-
taneously provide a wide field of view to cap-
ture collective thermodynamic phenomena and
enable statistically robust analysis, while main-
taining the near-atomic spatial resolution required
to resolve individual phases. Yet, conventional
analytical techniques present inherent trade-offs.
Bulk averaging methods such as X-ray and neu-
tron scattering deliver highly precise crystallo-
graphic information over large volumes but lack
the spatial resolution needed to resolve nanoscale
heterogeneity[7, 8]. Conversely, localized probes
including atomic force microscopy-infrared spec-
troscopy (AFM-IR) and high-resolution transmis-
sion electron microscopy (HRTEM), resolve struc-
tural features down to the atomic scale but are
typically restricted to limited viewing areas[9]. In
such case, overcoming the challenges would require
new and robust analytical techniques that can
decouples the trade-off between field of view and
structural precision[10, 11].
Recently,
four-dimensional
scanning
trans-
mission
electron
microscopy
(4D-STEM)
has
emerged as a powerful route toward resolving the
challenges[10, 11]. By raster scanning a convergent
electron probe across a sample, a high-speed pixe-
lated detector records a complete two-dimensional
diffraction pattern at each probe position during
the process as shown in Fig. 1a. This gener-
ates a position-resolved, 4D diffraction data cube
from which local crystallinity, phase distribution,
and structural defect can be reconstructed[12–
14]. Furthermore, the integration of direct electron
detectors has expanded the practical scope of 4D-
STEM by enabling rapid acquisition, low-dose
operation, and larger-area mapping. In addition,
because scattering information is recorded inde-
pendently at each scan coordinate, 4D-STEM can
survey comparatively large spatial areas while pre-
serving the nanoscale sampling resolution dictated
by the probe size and scanning step[10, 14]. There-
fore, 4D-STEM is by nature an ideal technique for
probing the complex material interfaces.
Despite
these
advantages,
the
application
of 4D-STEM, particularly for high-throughput
industrial
workflows,
remains
constrained
by
the
absence
of
automated
data
analysis
architectures[15–18].
This
is
because
modern
direct
electron
detectors
capture
diffraction
patterns (DPs) at kilohertz-to-tens-of-kilohertz
frame rates, routinely generating raw datasets
ranging from tens to hundreds of gigabytes during
a single scan, equivalent to millions of DPs[15–
17]. Consequently, the scale and dimensionality
of this data far exceed the limits of tradi-
tional analysis creating a severe computational
bottleneck[15, 16].
To address this issue, recent studies have
employed
unsupervised
machine-learning
approaches, including non-negative matrix fac-
torization and hierarchical clustering, to segment
and interpret 4D-STEM datasets[19–21]. While
these methods group mathematically similar pat-
terns and reveal spatially coherent domains, they
do
not
provide
deterministic
crystallographic
labels without subsequent manual interpretation
or comparison with diffraction databases[20–22].
Furthermore, such workflows often depend on
multi-step offline pipelines involving preprocess-
ing, dimensionality reduction, clustering, and
qualitative evaluation, with performance that is
highly sensitive to user-specified hyperparameters
such as the expected number of clusters[21, 22].
These limitations are particularly remarkable
2


when DPs overlap or subtle structural differences
must be distinguished in complex heterogeneous
systems.
We believe establishing a fully autonomous
end-to-end supervised architecture for phase iden-
tification in 4D-STEM dataset is currently hin-
dered by three major reasons[23]. First, physical-
informed deep learning is constrained by a pro-
nounced simulation-to-experiment domain gap,
as experimental datasets exhibit dynamical scat-
tering, point defects, thickness variations, and
low-dose Poisson noise that cause models trained
exclusively on synthetic simulations to degrade
upon experimental transfer[23–26]. Second, stan-
dard convolutional neural network (CNN) archi-
tectures lack the physical inductive biases to
handle geometric symmetries. This is saying they
could misidentify the rotational variance of Bragg
reflections under arbitrary crystal orientations as
distinct structural phases[27, 28]. Third, the trans-
mitted electron diffraction along the z-axis means
that complex interfaces project overlapping sig-
natures from mixed or disordered phases into a
single diffraction pattern. This projection violates
the mutually exclusive and single-label assump-
tion of traditional classification networks, which
highlights a critical need for information-theoretic
uncertainty metrics to quantify structural diver-
sity beyond discrete classification.
Here, we introduce Multi4D, an end-to-end
neural network workflow designed to automate
sub-nanometer multi-component phase mapping.
Multi4D resolves the Sim2real domain gap by inte-
grating a physics-constrained, latent-space Diffu-
sion Transformer (DiT) that translates idealized
simulations into experimental-style patterns while
preserving crystallographic geometry[29, 30]. Con-
currently, it employs a rotation-invariant coor-
dinate convolutional neural network (RIC-CNN)
backbone to enforce geometric symmetry, facil-
itating orientation agnostic phase identification
without rotational data augmentation[31]. We
further introduce diffraction-inferred structural
complexity (DISC), an entropy-based descrip-
tor derived from classifier probability outputs
designed to quantify local structural ambiguity
in complex interfaces. We demonstrate the effi-
cacy of Multi4D across standard nanoparticle
benchmarks and three experimental interfacial
systems ranging from a commercial superconduct-
ing heterostructure, a corroded magnesium-alloy
interface, and a moisture-degraded, sulfur-based
solid-electrolyte interface.
Results
Overview of Multi4D
Multi4D is an end-to-end multi-component clas-
sification framework for 4D-STEM data, as sum-
marized in Fig. 1b. The overall architecture takes
as input an unlabeled experimental diffraction
dataset and a candidate set of crystal structures
represented by crystallographic information files
(CIFs) (see Supplementary Table 1). These candi-
date structures are enumerated from prior knowl-
edge, complementary characterization, or physi-
cally motivated hypotheses regarding the interface
composition. Multi-slice simulations are then per-
formed over random crystal orientations to gen-
erate a labeled diffraction dataset. By integrating
experimental measurements with the simulation-
derived prior, Multi4D assigns each diffraction
pattern to one candidate component and thereby
produces a spatially resolved component map of
the interface.
It is worth to emphasize that before entering
the neural network workflow, experimental DPs
are preprocessed to correct tractable experimen-
tal variations. This preprocessing includes beam-
center
and
elliptical-distortion
corrections[32],
together with denoising[26], to reduce system-
atic geometric artifacts and experimental noise
while preserving the diffraction features required
for downstream domain alignment and multi-
component classification. After these corrections,
the DPs are examined for signals beyond the
transmitted central disk. Probe positions showing
neither off-center diffraction spots nor a diffuse
amorphous ring is treated as empty, while patterns
containing only an amorphous ring and no discrete
Bragg reflections are labeled as purely amorphous.
These patterns are removed before candidate-
component analysis. Because this screening is
based on directly observable diffraction features,
rather than a learned model, the neural network
can focus on the more difficult task of identifying
overlapping crystalline components in heteroge-
neous regions.

shown in Fig. 2b. For each simulated input, the
model uses attention-based matching in a global
feature space to retrieve the most similar experi-
mental reference.
The retrieved examples thus guide the trans-
lation toward the target experimental domain
by capturing its specific imaging characteristics,
without introducing arbitrary peak displacements.
To further constrain the process within a physi-
cal framework, both simulated and experimental
DPs are encoded into a shared latent space using
a diffraction-specialized autoencoder.
A retrieval-conditioned latent Diffusion Trans-
former
then
performs
the
translation
under
explicit physics-based constraints. Within this
shared latent space, dual style embeddings encode
complementary spatial and textural characteris-
tics of experimental diffraction, including back-
ground statistics, dynamical scattering, and inten-
sity distributions (Fig. 2d). The translation is fur-
ther constrained by Gram loss and peak-value L1
loss, which align higher-order appearance statis-
tics with the experimental domain while preserv-
ing Bragg peak geometry (Fig. 2e). In this way, the
Sim2real module achieves domain alignment while
maintaining the structural fidelity of the simulated
DPs.
The second module of Multi4D performs final
component recognition using a rotation-invariant
classifier, termed RIC-CNN (Fig. 2e)[31]. This
module is designed to handle the strong rotational
variation commonly observed in DPs, which can
be difficult for standard vision models. Once the
patterns are recentered, rotating DPs usually indi-
cates a change in local crystal orientation rather
than a different material component. The classifier
must therefore remain insensitive to orientation
while retaining the Bragg-pattern features needed
to distinguish crystallographic components.
Specifically, RIC-CNN replaces standard con-
volutions with rotation-invariant coordinate con-
volutions defined with respect to the diffraction
centre (Fig. 2f)[31, 33]. Rather than relying on a
fixed Cartesian sampling grid, these operations are
formulated in a centre-referenced coordinate sys-
tem, allowing equivalent diffraction structures to
remain comparable under arbitrary in-plane rota-
tion. By stacking such rotation-aware operations,
RIC-CNN learns feature representations that are
robust to rotational variation while preserving
structurally informative diffraction geometry.
Together, these two modules define the core
logic
of
Multi4D.
Sim2real
aligns
simulated
diffraction patterns with the experimental domain
while preserving diffraction structure, whereas
RIC-CNN enables robust component recognition
despite rotational variation. Their integration
allows direct, automated, and spatially resolved
identification of local crystallographic components
in complex 4D-STEM datasets.

Sim2real and RIC-CNN modules

RIC-CNN. For Sim2real, domain alignment was
quantified using Fr´echet inception distance (FID),

Overall, Sim2real outperformed other models on
all three metrics of experimental-domain align-
ment, while ranking second to the simulated input
in source-consistency comparison. These results
confirm that Sim2real narrows the simulation–

Accurate multi-component
classification of experimental
4D-STEM diffraction patterns
We next benchmark Multi4D as an end-to-end
workflow on 4D-STEM dataset acquired from var-
ious nanoparticles comprising Au, ZnO, TiO2,
Pd, and Co3O4. This dataset serves as a stan-
dard test for crystallographic classification, as it
represents a diverse range of crystal structures
and varying nanoparticle morphologies under real-
istic conditions. On this experimental bench-
mark, Multi4D achieved a classification accu-
racy of 98.82%. Notably, the model occasionally
fails to correctly classify highly symmetric DPs,
representing a limitation in orientation-agnostic
mapping. Nonetheless, the representative results,
illustrated in Fig. 3a–d, show precise agreement
between the translated experimental diffraction
patterns and their simulated crystallographic tem-
plates along with accurate identification of the
nanoparticle boundaries.
To determine whether these performance gains
require our integrated architecture, we com-
pared the full Multi4D workflow against stan-
dard computer vision backbones and individual
ablation baselines under identical training condi-
tions. Specifically, we compared the full frame-
work against a conventional backbone trained
directly on simulated DPs, an RIC-CNN classi-
fier without Sim2real alignment, and a Sim2real-
enhanced backbone lacking RIC-CNN. The com-
plete Multi4D model achieved the highest accu-
racy and macro F1 score on the five-class bench-
mark shown in Fig. 3e,f, demonstrating that
neither domain alignment nor rotation-invariant
recognition alone is sufficient. A robust multi-
component classification requires the synergistic
integration of both modules within a unified work-
flow.
Beyond peak performance, we examined how
each model responded to increasing amounts of
simulated training data in Fig. 3g. The full
Sim2real + RIC-CNN workflow achieved the high-
est experimental-domain accuracy at every train-
ing fraction, with the clearest advantage in the
low-data regime. Trained on the same Sim2real-
aligned dataset, RIC-CNN substantially outper-
formed ConvNeXtV2 when only 5% of the training
data were available and remained more accurate
as the dataset increased, although the gap grad-
ually narrowed. Because the domain-alignment
component was identical in this comparison, the
result points to the classifier architecture as the
main source of the difference. In particular, the
rotation-invariant design of RIC-CNN is better
suited to the rotational variability of diffraction
patterns, enabling the network to learn crystal-
lographic features from fewer training examples
than a general-purpose vision backbone.
A different trend emerged when the classi-
fiers were trained directly on unaligned simulated
data. Neither model showed a consistent improve-
ment in experimental-domain accuracy as more
simulated data were added. Instead, their per-
formance fluctuated and declined at larger train-
ing fractions, indicating negative scaling under
simulation-to-experiment domain shift. This pat-
tern is consistent with the models becoming
increasingly dependent on simulation-specific cues
that do not transfer reliably to experimental
diffraction patterns. Although the result does not
by itself prove simulation-domain overfitting, it
shows that increasing the amount of unaligned
simulated data is not sufficient to improve cross-
domain
generalization
and
may
even
reduce
it. Taken together, these findings indicate that
Sim2real alignment is necessary for additional
simulated data to translate into better experi-
mental performance, while the diffraction-specific
design of RIC-CNN provides a further advantage,
particularly when training data are limited.
Resolving a complex
superconducting interface with
Multi4D
We further apply the Multi4D on a commer-
cialized superconductor device with structurally
complex superconducting interface that dictate
its performance. Unlike the controlled benchmark
8


setting of which the sample is structurally sim-
ple in Fig. 4, the focus ion beam (FIB) cross-
section of the device exhibits highly heteroge-
neous landscape with crystalline and amorphous
regions mixed at nanoscale. As shown in Fig. 4,
Multi4D resolves different phases across the inter-
face, identifying an upper CeO2 layer, ultrathin
LaMnO3/MgO interfacial regions, a buried Y2O3
layer, and a lower amorphous Al-containing layer.
More important, a close examination of the
interface shows an unprecedented spatial resolu-
tion down to single pixel that is approximately 1
nm2. Within the Y2O3 layer, Multi4D successfully
identifies several embedded CeO2 nanodomains in
Fig. 4c. These nanoscale features are difficult to
resolve using conventional imaging techniques and
fall below the detection limit of energy-dispersive
X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) due to their small vol-
ume fraction and projection overlap. Resolving
the embedded domains is of high physical and
technological significance because such secondary-
phase impurities can act as localized defects or
flux-pinning centers deteriorate the superconduct-
ing property. This phase mapping is supported by
three validations. First, the reconstructed compo-
nents are consistent with prior structural knowl-
edge and the real-space Z-contrast observed in the
bright-field image. Second, representative diffrac-
tion patterns extracted from each layer closely
match the simulated diffraction patterns of the
corresponding crystal structures, while the lower
Al-containing layer exhibits the diffuse halo char-
acteristic of an amorphous AlOX phase. Third, the
classification is corroborated by energy dispersive
X-ray spectrometer (EDX) elemental mapping in
Fig. 4d and e, where Ce is enriched in the upper
layer, La, Mn and Mg are localized near the buffer
interface, Y is concentrated in the seed layer,
and Al and O dominate the lower region. This
high consistency among structural prior knowl-
edge, diffraction-pattern matching, and chemical
mapping provides strong orthogonal validation for
the recovered phase assignments.
Taken together, these results demonstrate that
Multi4D can robustly disentangle multiple adja-
cent phases in a real superconducting interface
rather than only in standard reference specimens,
extending the validation of the framework to
experimentally heterogeneous engineering materi-
als. Notably, within the reconstructed phase map-
ping in Fig. 4b, while bulk-like regions are assigned
as well-defined single phases, the interfacial transi-
tion zones exhibit remarkable local variation with
overlapping yet faint Bragg peaks as shown in Fig.
4c. This indicates that hard phase labels alone
are insufficient to fully describe the structural
heterogeneity along the projection direction. This
physical limitation of standard classification mod-
els motivates the introduction of a quantitative
descriptor for local interfacial complexity in the
following section.
Predictive uncertainty reveals local
interfacial structural complexity
Although the superconductor served as an effec-
tive model system, its diffraction patterns at each
probe position typically originate from only a sin-
gle constituent. In more complex heterogeneous
interfaces, overlapping diffraction patterns from
multiple phases along the beam direction (z-axis)
are frequently observed, which poses a major chal-
lenge for resolving individual components. How-
ever, Multi4D provides a mathematically natural
route to quantify this complexity because the clas-
sifier outputs a probability distribution over the
physically plausible candidate phases. Rather than
treating this distribution solely as a confidence
score, we interpret its spread as predictive uncer-
tainty within the candidate structural space. To
project this uncertainty into a spatially resolved
descriptor of interfacial heterogeneity, we define
diffraction-inferred structural complexity (DISC)
as the normalized predictive entropy of the classi-
fier output.

Generalization across diverse
interfacial material systems
To extend the generality of Multi4D as a uni-
fied framework for complex interfacial analysis, we
further applied it to material systems with fun-
damentally different chemistries, crystallographic
motifs, and degrees of structural heterogene-
ity. Beyond the superconducting interface, we
examined two representative cases: a corroded
Mg-alloy interface and a degraded sulfur-based
solid-electrolyte interface. Together, these systems
encompass markedly different crystal structures
and interfacial morphologies, providing a strin-
gent test of the framework’s transferability across
material classes. In both cases, Multi4D produced
spatially coherent component classification maps
and corresponding DISC distributions. This con-
sistent performance across distinct material envi-
ronments indicates that the framework is not lim-
ited to a particular chemical system or interfacial
geometry.
As shown in the Fig. 5, for the Mg-alloy
case, we examined corrosion products formed on
a stainless magnesium alloy (Mg–11 wt.% Y–1
wt.% Al) after 1.5 h of exposure to a 3.5 wt.%
NaCl solution. The cross-sectional structure con-
sists of the protective W layer deposited during
focused ion beam preparation, a surface corrosion-
product layer, an amorphous passivation layer,
and the underlying substrate from top to bottom
in Fig. 5a. Multi4D identifies the surface corro-
sion products as primarily MgO and Mg(OH)2
with good crystallinity, as indicated by the red
and cyan regions in the classification map. An
enlarged view of region 1 is shown in Fig. 5b
overlays representative experimental diffraction
patterns with the Bragg reflections identified by
the framework, confirming the accuracy of phase
assignment. Similarly, in region 2 from Fig. 5c,
Multi4D detected diffraction signatures of MgO
alongside the primary magnesium lattice, indicat-
ing localized oxidation during sample preparation
or environmental exposure. These results demon-
strate that the framework distinguishes multiple
adjacent corrosion products and maps their spatial
distribution across the interface.
We also applied Multi4D to observe moisture-
induced degradation process of sulfur-based argy-
rodite solid electrolyte, which is a widely used
solid-state battery material as shown in Fig. 6.
The framework successfully resolves a complex
degradation layer, identifying the spatial distri-
butions of eight distinct decomposition products
including hydrated lithium compounds, phospho-
rus sulfides, metal sulfides, and lithium chloride
derivatives that previous not possible to observe.
Notably, the amorphous fraction, which is typi-
cally overlooked in conventional crystallographic
mapping, was reliably separated and mapped
along grain boundaries and near the exposed
surface. The spatial mapping reveals the correla-
tion among diverse degradation products, poten-
tially providing insight into the reaction sequence
and the role of local structural heterogeneity in
facilitating water penetration along the moisture-
driven failure pathway.
We want to point out that the spatial distri-
bution of DISC reveals a consistent qualitative
trend: bulk regions far from interfaces exhibit low
structural complexity, whereas phase boundaries,
degradation fronts, and transition zones display
significantly elevated complexity. This observation
is reasonable as single-phase bulk regions yield
stable, highly defined diffraction signatures, while
transition layers contain overlapping or locally
perturbed Bragg reflections that broaden the clas-
sifier’s probability distribution. The recurrence of
this spatial trend across diverse material systems
indicates that DISC is not an artifact of a specific
dataset, but a generalizable physical descriptor
of local structural ambiguity. By jointly mapping
dominant phases and quantifying structural com-
plexity, the framework captures the spatial extent
and internal disorder of interfacial transition zones
where discrete classification labels are insufficient.
In conclusion, these results demonstrate that
Multi4D
generalizes
effectively
across
diverse
material classes, establishing a unified frame-
work for multi-component identification and spa-
tially resolved structural complexity analysis. By
combining physics-preserving style translation,
rotation-invariant coordinate convolutions, and
predictive entropy mapping, Multi4D extends 4D-
STEM from qualitative visualization to robust,
quantitative analysis of complex material inter-
faces.

Discussion
Multi4D
demonstrates
that
large
4D-STEM
datasets can be converted into spatially coher-
ent multi-component maps without reducing the
analysis to manual pattern matching or purely
data-driven clustering. By linking experimental
diffraction signals to a physically constrained
structural library, it produces assignments with
direct crystallographic meaning across interfaces
containing several adjacent components. This is
the main practical advance of the classification
framework: it preserves a discrete, interpretable
map while remaining scalable to datasets that
would be difficult to examine pattern by pattern.
The resulting label, however, represents the dom-
inant candidate at each probe position. It should
not be read as a complete decomposition of every
structure contributing along the beam direction.
This distinction changes how the classification
map should be interpreted. A discrete label can
identify the dominant component even when the
diffraction signal is not uniquely described by that
component alone. DISC supplies the missing con-
text by indicating how strongly the selected class
is preferred over the other physically plausible
candidates. The recurring concentration of ele-
vated DISC around interfacial transition regions
suggests that these regions are better understood
13


as spatially extended zones of assignment non-
uniqueness rather than as perfectly sharp bound-
aries between discrete phases. Multi-component
classification and DISC therefore serve comple-
mentary purposes: one organizes the most likely
structural identities, while the other marks where
a single-label representation becomes incomplete.
There are important limits to this interpreta-
tion. DISC depends on the composition of the can-
didate library and on the calibration of the clas-
sifier probabilities. An elevated value cannot, on
its own, tell whether the underlying cause is phase
coexistence, local disorder, experimental noise,
domain shift, or similarity among candidate struc-
tures. Conversely, a low value does not establish
that an assignment is correct when the relevant
structure is missing from the library or the model
is overconfident. DISC should not be used to esti-
mate phase fractions, and comparisons between
experiments require comparable candidate spaces
and probability calibration. These constraints are
particularly relevant to the present single-label
implementation. Although an amorphous-like cat-
egory can be recognized from diffuse scattering,
the framework does not yet resolve distinct forms
of short-range order or separate strongly over-
lapping crystalline signals into their constituent
contributions. Multi-label mixture models, repre-
sentations tailored to diffuse scattering, and a ded-
icated out-of-library score would address different
aspects of these limitations.
The practical value of combining the two
outputs is that classification provides a work-
ing structural map, while DISC indicates where
that map deserves closer examination. Regions
with non-unique assignments can then be pri-
oritized for complementary spectroscopy, higher-
resolution acquisition, additional simulations, or
revision of the candidate library. Predictive uncer-
tainty becomes part of the experimental decision
process rather than a by-product of classifica-
tion. With improved probability calibration and
explicit detection of structures outside the can-
didate space, each map could inform the next
measurement or model update. The longer-term
contribution of Multi4D may therefore lie not
only in accelerating multi-component mapping,
but also in showing where the current structural
interpretation requires additional evidence.
